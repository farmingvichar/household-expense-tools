How to calculate your monthly living expenses

Whether you are building a simple monthly budget based on salary, checking how much you need for a 3–6 month emergency fund, or planning a move, the cleanest way to start is to pull up your last two bank and credit card statements. Separate fixed bills that stay the same each month (rent, car payment, internet) from variable costs that shift week to week (groceries, gas, utilities).

Total Monthly Expenses = Fixed Monthly Bills + Variable Living Costs

Remaining Balance = Monthly Take-Home Income − Total Monthly Expenses

Expense Percentage = (Total Monthly Expenses ÷ Monthly Take-Home Income) × 100

Annual Spending = Total Monthly Expenses × 12

Emergency Fund Target (3–6 Months) = Total Monthly Expenses × 3 to 6

Worked example: budgeting $4,500 a month

Suppose your household brings home $4,500 after taxes each month and has the following living costs: $1,500 rent, $220 utilities, $130 phone and internet, $380 auto/student loans, $550 groceries, $260 gas and transit, $240 insurance, and $320 in other expenses:

  • Total monthly expenses: $3,600.00 per month ($43,200.00 per year).
  • Remaining balance: $4,500 − $3,600 = $900.00 left over each month.
  • Expense percentage: ($3,600 ÷ $4,500) × 100 = 80.0% spent, leaving a 20.0% savings rate.
  • Emergency fund range: 3 months = $10,800; 6 months = $21,600.

Across all US households, housing averages roughly $2,316/month (~33.9% of spending) and transportation averages around $1,124/month (~16.5%).

50/30/20 vs. 70/20/10 budget rules

When you enter your monthly take-home pay above, the calculator automatically displays the classic 50/30/20 rule: roughly 50% for essential needs (housing, groceries, utilities, minimum debt payments), 30% for wants (dining out, streaming, travel), and 20% for savings or extra debt payoff.

If housing takes up a larger share of your paycheck—which is common on a $2,000 to $3,500 monthly budget or in higher-cost metro areas—the 70/20/10 rule can feel more realistic: keep total living expenses (needs plus wants) near 70%, direct 20% to savings, and put 10% toward debt reduction or personal goals.

Frequently Asked Questions

Monthly Expense & Budget FAQs

Clear answers on expense formulas, take-home pay, US household averages, and handling irregular bills.

What is the formula for calculating monthly expenses?

Add all fixed monthly bills (housing, utilities, phone, debt payments, insurance) and variable living costs (groceries, gas, personal spending) together: Total Monthly Expenses = Fixed Bills + Variable Expenses. Then subtract that total from your monthly take-home pay to find your remaining balance.

Should I enter gross income or net take-home pay?

Enter your net take-home pay—the actual amount deposited into your bank account after taxes, health insurance, and payroll deductions. Budgeting from take-home pay shows your real cash flow and prevents overestimating what is left to spend or save.

What are the average monthly expenses for one person or a family in the US?

According to recent US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Consumer Expenditure Survey data, the average American household spends about $6,825 per month ($81,905 per year), up from $6,545 per month ($78,535 per year) in 2024. Typical monthly spending runs roughly $4,066 to $4,641 for a single person, $7,341 for a married couple without children, and $8,809 to $9,780 for families with children, with housing (~33.9%) and transportation (~16.5%) as the two largest costs.

What is the difference between the 50/30/20 and 70/20/10 budget rules?

The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home pay into 50% essential needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings or extra debt payoff. The 70/20/10 rule is a helpful alternative in high-cost areas: 70% covers all living expenses (needs plus wants), 20% goes to savings or investing, and 10% goes toward debt payoff or giving.

How do I account for annual, quarterly, or irregular bills?

Divide annual bills by 12 and quarterly bills by 3—such as car insurance renewals, vehicle registration, property taxes, or holiday costs—and include that monthly share inside the Other expenses field so irregular bills do not disrupt your monthly budget.

Can I calculate my monthly bills without entering income, and is my data saved?

Yes. If you leave income blank, the calculator still totals your monthly expenses, annual spending, biggest category, and 3 to 6 month emergency fund range. All math runs strictly inside your browser—nothing is saved, uploaded, or tracked.
