How It Works

This calculator measures the real cash difference between buying prepared restaurant meals and cooking equivalent portions at home. First, it multiplies your weekly restaurant meals by the number of people eating and your average pre-tax menu price. Next, it applies your combined tax, tip, and delivery fee percentage—because a $16 entrée rarely leaves your bank account at $16 once local sales tax, an 18%–20% tip, or third-party app service charges are added.

To convert weekly habits into an accurate monthly figure, the math uses 4.333 weeks per month (the same standard used by the U.S. Department of Agriculture in its monthly food reports) rather than assuming every month has exactly four weeks. Finally, it subtracts your estimated per-serving home grocery cost from your total restaurant spend to show your exact monthly and annual savings. All arithmetic runs in integer cents inside your browser so your numbers stay private and never leave your device.

What the Result Means

Monthly & Annual Savings: This is the net cash remaining in your household budget if you replace those specific restaurant or takeout meals with home-cooked dishes. Even swapping just three $18 lunches a week for $4.50 homemade meals frees up roughly $2,100 over twelve months.

The "Convenience Multiplier": Across U.S. households, dining out typically costs 2.5x to 3.5x more per plate than cooking from scratch. When delivery app markups and service fees are included, that multiplier often crosses 4x.

Compounding Opportunity: If you redirect $250 per month in saved takeout spending into a 4.25% High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA) or broad index fund, that single habit change equals more than $16,500 in five years.

Practical Ways to Lower Your Food Bill Without Giving Up Convenience

You do not need to cook every single meal from scratch to capture most of these savings:

  1. Cut the highest-fee meals first: Delivery orders carry layered costs—menu markups (often 15%–30% higher than dine-in prices), service fees, delivery fees, and driver tips. Picking up takeout yourself or reserving delivery for rare occasions immediately eliminates 20%–35% in friction costs.
  2. Batch-cook lunches, keep dinners flexible: Weekday lunches are usually eaten at a desk out of habit rather than social enjoyment. Doubling a Sunday dinner recipe to pack three weekday lunches routinely saves $45–$60 per person every week.
  3. Audit grocery food waste: According to USDA estimates, the average American household discards roughly 30% of the food it buys. Buying ingredients for two to three planned meals instead of an aspirational week-long haul prevents spoiled produce from eroding your home-cooking savings.

Limitations

This tool compares direct ingredient and menu outlays and does not assign a dollar value to your grocery shopping, prep, or cleanup time. It also assumes you already own basic kitchen cookware and pantry staples (oil, salt, spices), and it does not adjust for household food waste if unused groceries spoil before being cooked.

This tool does not provide financial, tax, legal, investment, or accounting advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a home-cooked meal cost on average in the U.S.?

Based on the USDA Cost of Food Reports, cooking at home costs between $2.75 and $6.30 per person per meal, depending on whether your household shops on a Thrifty, Low-Cost, Moderate, or Liberal grocery plan. Most two-to-four-person households land right around $4.25 to $5.00 per serving when mixing pantry staples, fresh produce, and standard proteins like chicken, eggs, or beans.

What percentage should I enter for tax, tip, and delivery fees?

For casual counter pickup or dine-in meals, 22% to 28% is realistic in most U.S. states (combining 6%–9% sales tax with a 15%–20% tip). If you frequently order through DoorDash, Uber Eats, or Grubhub, enter 35% to 50% to account for higher in-app menu prices, service fees, delivery charges, and driver tips.

Why does the calculator use 4.333 weeks per month instead of 4?

A 52-week year divided by 12 months equals 4.333 weeks per month. Multiplying weekly meal habits by 4 undercounts your true annual restaurant spending by an entire month (48 weeks instead of 52).

Does cooking at home still save money if I live alone?

Yes, though single-person households pay roughly 20% more per serving than a family of four due to smaller package sizes and higher spoilage risk. Cooking recipes that freeze well—such as chili, stir-fries, or grain bowls—keeps solo meal costs near $4.50–$5.50 per serving compared to $16+ for takeout.

Are my meal numbers or grocery budgets stored anywhere?

No. Every calculation executes locally inside your browser using JavaScript. We do not collect, transmit, or store your financial inputs on any server.
